## Claim detail

**1. Referred pain, forehead/temples.** Harvard Health says brain freeze is usually felt in the forehead or temples, triggered by cold on the roof of the mouth or back of the throat, and that the brain misreads the signal as coming from the head (referred pain). Johns Hopkins also calls it referred pain. ICHD-3 criterion 4.5.2 requires frontal or temporal headache right after a cold stimulus to the palate and/or posterior pharyngeal wall. Three independent sources agree. SUPPORTED.

**2. "Back of your throat sits a bundle of nerves."** The sphenopalatine (pterygopalatine) ganglion sits in the pterygopalatine fossa, a deep space behind the nose, hanging off the maxillary nerve (Matharu et al., UCL repository). It is not in the throat. The cold-sensing endings on the palate and pharynx are branches of the trigeminal nerve (palate) and other cranial nerves, not one throat "bundle". Cleveland Clinic only names the term "sphenopalatine ganglioneuralgia"; Harvard and Hopkins name the trigeminal nerve, not the SPG. The "bundle at the back of the throat" is a plain-English blur of two things. NOT SUPPORTED as worded; fix by placing the nerves on the roof of the mouth (where every source puts the trigger).

**3. "Guarding the blood vessels that feed your brain."** The SPG does send parasympathetic fibers, and one paper cites evidence for input to cerebral vessels, but the sources on brain freeze describe nerves near the palate vessels, not a guard of the brain's own supply. No institutional source describes it that way. UNCERTAIN, overstated. Drop it.

**4. Constriction vs dilation; protective purpose.** Sources differ. Harvard Health: leading theory is that small vessels at the palate narrow, then quickly widen. Hopkins: vessels narrow to protect core temperature, then reopen quickly and send the pain signal. Cleveland Clinic: vessels widen (no constriction mentioned). Serrador et al. 2012 (13 volunteers, ice water against the palate): blood flow in the anterior cerebral artery rose sharply as pain peaked, then the artery narrowed as pain faded. The authors themselves speculate that the dilation protects the brain by sending warm blood, and that the sudden pressure rise causes the pain. They describe these as ideas needing more research. EBSCO notes scientists are not certain and several theories compete. So: "constriction then rapid dilation, with pain tied to the dilation" is the best-supported picture; "protecting the brain" is a hypothesis. The v1 sentence "slams those vessels shut" alone leaves out the rush back open, which is when the pain hits. UNCERTAIN.

**5. Same line as the forehead (trigeminal).** Harvard Health (two pages) says pain signals go to the trigeminal nerve, which relays them to the brain. Hopkins says the pain signal travels through the trigeminal nerve and is felt in forehead and temples. The forehead is served by the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve (standard anatomy; not separately cited here). Fine as a simplification, but "that nerve bundle" refers back to the wrong structure (see 2). SUPPORTED AS SIMPLIFICATION.

**6. "Clerical error."** Fair, punchy gloss for referred pain: real pain, misplaced location. Hopkins and Harvard use that exact concept. Note the sources say the brain "misreads" the signal, which is a theory about how referred pain works rather than a settled mechanism. SUPPORTED AS SIMPLIFICATION.

**7. The fix.** Cleveland Clinic and Hopkins both advise pressing the tongue or thumb to the roof of the mouth; Harvard mentions the curled tongue. But Harvard says no treatment is proven and experts do not agree on the best one, and Hopkins says it does not explain why pressing works. Cleveland Clinic's framing is warmth transfer. No source says the vessels "reopen" as the relief mechanism (under the dilation theory the vessels are already open at peak pain). Remedy: widely recommended, unproven. Reason: NOT SUPPORTED. Hedge it.

**8. "Ten seconds."** No source gives 10 seconds for relief from the thumb trick. Natural duration: Harvard says typically within two minutes; Cleveland Clinic says a few seconds to two minutes; EBSCO says about 20 to 60 seconds, up to two minutes; Hopkins says usually under five minutes; ICHD-3 allows up to 10 minutes after the cold stimulus is removed. "Ten seconds" is below the typical range and would also be indistinguishable from natural resolution. NOT SUPPORTED.

**9. "Your brain is not cold."** The brain is not being chilled; the trigger is cold at the palate/pharynx and the pain is referred. No source says the brain temperature drops. SUPPORTED AS SIMPLIFICATION (the Serrador authors do speculate about protecting the brain from cooling, but that is a hypothesis about the response, not evidence that the brain is cold).

## NARRATION_v2 (proposed) - 125 words

That stab of pain behind your forehead when you eat ice cream too fast? Your brain is not cold. It's being lied to.

On the roof of your mouth sits a web of cold-sensing nerves and vessels. When something freezing hits, the vessels slam shut, then rush back open. Scientists suspect that swing protects the brain from a chill.

The catch: those nerves share the trigeminal line your forehead uses. So your brain reads the alarm and points at the wrong address — your forehead. The pain is real. The location is a clerical error.

The fix: press your warm thumb or tongue to the roof of your mouth. Warmth seems to calm the alarm, though nobody's sure why. Most brain freezes fade within two minutes.

(Word count measured with `wc -w`: 125. Upper edge of the 105-125 range; "trigeminal" is a 4-syllable word for the voice-over, check pronunciation/timing.)

## What I could not verify

- The Serrador abstract itself (FASEB J supplement) was not read; I relied on press coverage of it (ScienceDaily, EurekAlert). Sample size (13) and the authors' hedged wording come from those summaries.
- NCBI StatPearls and PMC pages on the SPG were blocked by a browser check, so SPG anatomy rests on one paper (UCL repository), not an institutional page.
- No source I read explicitly says the SPG carries brain freeze pain. Harvard, Hopkins and EBSCO name the trigeminal nerve. The SPG link (Cleveland Clinic's term "sphenopalatine ganglioneuralgia") is a naming convention, not a verified pathway.
- The forehead being served by the trigeminal nerve is standard anatomy; I did not pull a separate citation.
- Why pressing the tongue or thumb works: no source gives a tested mechanism.
- The Hopkins page and the Harvard pages fetched via a summarizing tool; I paraphrased and did not read the full page text.
